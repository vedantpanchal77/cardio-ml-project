02

Training the model

Logistic regression, implemented without scikit-learn (ml/logistic_regression.py). Features are standardised using the training split's mean and standard deviation only, then batch gradient descent minimises cross-entropy loss with a small L2 penalty. Training runs once on the server (python train.py); the API serves the saved weights.

The update rule

For every epoch, the model predicts a probability for all the training rows, measures the error, and nudges each weight against the gradient:

p = 1 / (1 + e−(w·x + b))
∂L/∂w = Xᵀ(p − y) / n + λw/n
w ← w − α · ∂L/∂w

08

Notes on the method

Why logistic regression

  • It outputs a probability, not just a label, so the threshold can be tuned after training for the cost of each kind of mistake.
  • Every weight is readable. The contribution bars above come straight out of the model, with no separate explainability layer bolted on.
  • It is cheap to train: a thousand full passes over the training rows take a few seconds of vectorised NumPy, so retraining after a change is painless.
  • It has one obvious failure mode: it can only draw a straight boundary. That ceiling shows in the comparison table.

Honest limitations

  • Blood pressure dominates the model, and in this dataset it is self-reported at a single visit. One nervous reading moves the prediction a long way.
  • Smoking and alcohol carry small negative weights, which is the opposite of the medical literature. That is confounding, not a discovery: these columns are under-reported and correlate with other things in the data.
  • The label is study-defined presence of cardiovascular disease at examination time, not a future event, so this predicts a current state, not a risk over years.
  • Accuracy sits in a narrow band for every method tried. The ceiling is in the features available, not in the choice of algorithm.
